This article is for general information only and is not financial advice or a recommendation to open an account or invest. It covers UK rules as at 31 October 2026.

Put £4,000 into a Lifetime ISA over a tax year and the government can add £1,000. It’s a source of government support that’s easy to overlook when you’re saving for a first home or for later life. The part worth understanding before you pay in is what happens if you need the money sooner or the home you want costs more than £450,000.

What is a Lifetime ISA?

A Lifetime ISA, or LISA, is a UK tax-free account for buying your first home or saving for later life. You can generally open one if you’re UK-resident and aged 18 to 39, and you need to make your first payment before you turn 40. You can pay in up to £4,000 each tax year until you turn 50. The government adds 25p for every £1 you put in, up to £1,000 per tax year. You can start with less than £4,000 and still get the same percentage bonus.

Your contributions count towards the overall £20,000 ISA allowance for 2026–27. The government bonus is separate. At 50, contributions and new bonuses stop, but the account stays open and can still earn interest or investment returns. GOV.UK’s Lifetime ISA overview

Who might benefit?

First-time buyers. The bonus can go towards a deposit if you’re saving for your first UK home. To qualify, you generally need to have made your first LISA payment at least 12 months before the withdrawal, buy with an eligible mortgage, and intend to live there as your main home. Your provider sends the money to your conveyancer or solicitor. If you’re buying with someone who has owned a home before, you can still use your own LISA if you qualify. HMRC’s first-home purchase rules

People saving for later life. You can take money out without a government withdrawal charge from age 60, including if you already own a home. A LISA can sit alongside a pension. When comparing them, factor in any employer pension contributions and tax relief you could receive through a pension.

A cash LISA earns interest and avoids stock market swings, which can matter when you’re close to buying. A stocks and shares LISA can hold investments, which may suit a longer timeframe. Their value can fall, and you could get back less than you put in, even after the bonus.

What changed on 29 October 2026?

  • 0%: A qualifying first home costing £450,000 or less, withdrawals from age 60, and withdrawals for qualifying terminal illness with a life expectancy of less than 12 months.
  • 20%: From 29 October 2026, an otherwise qualifying first-home purchase above £450,000. The price cap for charge-free purchases stays at £450,000. The reduced rate applies to eligible withdrawals from that date.
  • 25%: Other unauthorised withdrawals, such as taking money out for an emergency before age 60. Moving money into a different type of ISA before age 60 also generally triggers this charge.

Say you put in £4,000 and the bonus takes your balance to £5,000. Ignoring interest, investment movements and fees, the 20% charge leaves you with £4,000. The 25% charge leaves you with £3,750, which is £250 less than you paid in. Both rates apply to the amount withdrawn, including any bonus and growth in that amount. Provider fees and transfers

Check the provider’s tariff separately. A stocks and shares LISA can involve platform fees, fund charges and dealing fees. Providers may also charge for transfers. Management fees paid directly from the LISA to the provider under the account terms do not trigger the government withdrawal charge. A formal transfer between LISAs also avoids the government withdrawal charge. Arrange it with the receiving provider. HMRC’s guidance on management fees · HMRC’s transfer rules

What’s changing for ISAs in 2027 and 2029?

April 2027. Earlier coverage reported a £12,000 cash ISA limit for under-65s and a 22% charge on interest paid on cash in certain investment ISAs. The October Budget reversed the package. The annual cash ISA limit is due to stay at £20,000, and the associated restrictions and interest charge are due to be dropped. These were separate from the LISA withdrawal rules. HMRC’s October 2026 savings update

April 2029. The government plans to replace the LISA for new savers with a First Time Buyer ISA. The proposed 25% bonus would be paid when the money goes towards a qualifying first home, up to a £20,000 total bonus. Existing LISA holders are expected to be able to keep using and contributing to their accounts under the existing rules. Budget 2026

The bonus is easiest to value when you have a clear idea of what the money is for and when you’ll need it. For a first home, the 12-month clock and likely purchase price matter. For later life, weigh the bonus against fees, access from age 60 and what a pension could offer.
